workdebugging

The bug that took four days to find

Spent most of this week chasing down an intermittent failure that only showed up under load, never in staging, never in any of our synthetic tests, just occasionally in production at the worst possible times. Classic heisenbug behavior, the kind where adding logging to try to catch it in the act makes it stop happening entirely, which is its own special kind of frustrating.

Turned out to be a race condition in a piece of code that had been sitting there, untouched and apparently fine, for over a year. Two goroutines occasionally hit a shared cache at exactly the wrong moment, and the fix ended up being about four lines once we actually found it. The finding part was the four days. The fixing part was twenty minutes.

Wrote up a full postmortem afterward, mostly for my own sake so I remember the debugging process next time something like this happens, because I guarantee I will have forgotten all of it by the time it does. Added a section specifically about the tools that actually helped versus the ones that just added noise, since half of debugging a problem like this is knowing which rabbit holes not to go down.

On a completely unrelated note, my keyboard has started making a faint clicking noise on one specific key that wasn't there a month ago. Investigating that feels like a much lower stakes version of the same debugging instinct, and yet somehow I'm avoiding it entirely.

techopinion

An unpopular opinion about build tools

Going to say something that will probably get some pushback if anyone from the frontend side of the org reads this: I think a lot of the tooling churn in the JavaScript ecosystem is self-inflicted, and every time I have to touch a frontend build pipeline for some internal tool, I come away more convinced of it. Not the frameworks themselves, those are mostly fine, but the sheer number of build tools, bundlers, and competing standards that all claim to solve the same problem slightly differently.

Backend has its own churn too, to be fair, I'm not pretending Python and Go are immune to fad-driven tooling decisions. But there's something particularly exhausting about opening a two-year-old frontend project and discovering that half the tooling has been deprecated in favor of something newer that solves the exact same problem with a slightly different config file format.

Anyway, had to build a tiny internal dashboard this week and ended up spending more time configuring the build step than writing the actual dashboard, which is exactly the kind of thing that makes me grateful I get to spend most of my time on the backend where things move a little slower and a little more deliberately.

Not sponsored by anyone, obviously. Just tired.

Why I even keep this thing updated

Someone asked me recently why I bother writing here at all, given that basically nobody reads it besides a handful of people who already know me. Fair question. The honest answer is that it's mostly for me, a low-stakes place to think through a week out loud without it disappearing into a group chat that nobody scrolls back through.

I tried keeping a private notes app for the same purpose a while back and it just didn't stick the same way. There's something about writing for an audience, even a mostly imaginary one, that makes me actually finish a thought instead of leaving it as three bullet points and moving on. Public accountability for an audience of basically nobody, which sounds like a contradiction but somehow works.

Also it's a decent record of small stuff I'd otherwise forget entirely. Looking back at posts from a year ago, half of what I wrote about I have zero independent memory of, which is either a little concerning or exactly the point of writing it down in the first place.

Anyway. Onward. More weekend project write-ups and fewer existential blogging questions starting next post, probably.

Might also start tagging posts a little more consistently. Going back through old ones there's a real mix of half-finished categorization that makes zero sense in hindsight. Low priority project, but a satisfying one for a slow weekend.

cookingfail

Bread, attempt number three

Tried making sourdough from scratch again and it came out dense enough to use as a doorstop. Did some reading afterward about why, and apparently altitude messes with rising times more than people expect, less air pressure means dough rises faster but weaker, so you either cut the proofing time or add more flour to compensate. Nobody warns you about this in the recipe, they just assume you're baking at sea level like everyone else in the universe.

Going to actually adjust the recipe properly next time instead of just being annoyed about it. Progress, allegedly.

In the meantime the dense, failed loaf has been repurposed as toast, which is honestly a pretty forgiving way to disguise a baking mistake. Butter fixes most things. Not structural integrity, apparently, but most things.

Ordering a kitchen scale next. Apparently eyeballing flour measurements is exactly the kind of imprecision that makes altitude-related baking problems even worse, and I've been doing exactly that for three attempts running.

Fourth attempt is scheduled for next weekend, assuming the scale actually arrives in time. Fingers crossed, though at this point I've started to suspect I just enjoy the ritual of trying more than I care about the actual bread.

workopinion

Async standups, a defense

Had a conversation this week with someone who's convinced daily standups only really work live, synchronous, everyone on camera at the same time. I get where that comes from, but with a team spread across three time zones, a live standup means someone's always either too early or too late, and eventually that turns into resentment nobody says out loud.

We moved to a written async format almost a year ago now, everyone posts what they did, what's next, and any blockers, in a shared channel by a set time each day. Took some adjustment, and a written update forces you to actually think through what you're saying rather than rambling for ninety seconds on camera, which I'd argue is a feature and not a bug.

Not saying live standups are wrong for every team, just that the assumption they're inherently better falls apart pretty quickly once you're not all sitting in overlapping working hours. Wrote up my case in more detail for anyone on the team still on the fence. We'll see if it changes any minds.

Curious to revisit this in another six months once the format's had more time to prove itself one way or the other, rather than judging it too early in either direction.
